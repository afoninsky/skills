I wouldn’t reproduce the competitor’s navigation, labels, or visual language without an approved source and rights review. Familiarity is a valid product goal, but close imitation creates avoidable intellectual-property, brand-confusion, and differentiation risks—and may preserve choices that do not fit our users.

I’d build the workflow from scratch using a clean-room approach:

1. Define the user’s job, entry points, key decisions, completion state, and recovery paths from our own research and product requirements.
2. Use established interface conventions rather than competitor-specific expressions: recognizable icons with text, clear action verbs, conventional back/next behavior, visible progress, and predictable placement of primary and secondary actions.
3. Create labels from our users’ vocabulary and domain model. Validate them with a short first-click or tree test rather than assuming the competitor’s terms are understood.
4. Apply our own design system—or establish a small, distinct foundation for type, color, spacing, shape, iconography, motion, and interaction states. Avoid copying distinctive compositions, illustrations, icon treatments, color combinations, or microcopy.
5. Design responsively around task priority:
   - Mobile: one primary task per screen, compact top bar, progressive disclosure, full-width actions, and persistent context only when essential.
   - Tablet: preserve the mobile flow while introducing a contextual panel where space improves comparison or review.
   - Desktop: use a stable task-oriented navigation and optional side panel; do not add columns unless they reduce switching or memory load.
6. Prototype the critical path and test it at representative breakpoints, including keyboard navigation, zoom/reflow, screen-reader naming, touch targets, errors, empty states, and interrupted-task recovery.

Before detailed visual design, I’d produce a task flow, information architecture, low-fidelity responsive wireframes, and a terminology table showing why each label exists. If stakeholders still want competitor parity, document the specific usability outcome they seek—such as lower learning time or easier switching—and test our clean-room design against that outcome. Any later use of competitor-derived material should wait for legal and brand approval with source provenance recorded.
