I would change this working model before starting. As written, it is likely to produce one over-polished idea rather than three meaningful alternatives.

Use one shared file for visibility, but give each designer a clearly owned, isolated page or branch. During the first round, they should work independently from the same problem statement, constraints, and evaluation rubric. Do not ask Direction 2 to improve Direction 1 yet; that anchors the second designer and turns an alternative into an iteration. Likewise, keeping the same dashboard skeleton is appropriate only if that structure is already a validated product constraint. Otherwise, require at least one direction to challenge the information architecture, not just the styling.

Do not recreate a published example pixel for pixel. Use references to identify transferable principles—hierarchy, density, navigation, interaction patterns—but produce an original design appropriate to this product, its users, and its content. Exact copying creates intellectual-property and brand risks and prevents the team from learning whether the reference actually fits the problem.

Suggested sequence:

1. Define success criteria and weight them: task completion, comprehension, accessibility, feasibility, differentiation, and consistency with the product.
2. Have all three designers create independent concepts, each documenting its key hypothesis and trade-offs.
3. Review concepts anonymously where practical. Score each criterion separately and retain every reviewer’s score and rationale; do not rely on a simple average, which can conceal disagreement and critical failures.
4. Identify the strongest direction based on evidence, then ask the team to improve it. Preserve coherent interaction and visual logic rather than merging attractive fragments into a “Frankenstein” design.
5. Prototype the leading direction and, if uncertainty remains, one meaningfully different challenger. Validate both with representative users or task-based usability checks.

The final choice should be a coherent system with traceable decisions—not an average of tastes. Merge a component only when it strengthens the chosen concept without breaking its underlying model, and record why it was adopted.
