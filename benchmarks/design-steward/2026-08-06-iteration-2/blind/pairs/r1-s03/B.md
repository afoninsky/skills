I wouldn’t reproduce the competitor’s navigation, labels, or visual language without an approved source and rights review. Familiarity is valuable, but copying those elements creates avoidable intellectual-property, trade-dress, and brand-confusion risk—and may preserve usability problems that do not fit your product.

We can still build a familiar workflow from scratch using common interaction conventions rather than competitor-specific expression:

- Start with user goals, core tasks, constraints, and content inventory.
- Create an original information architecture using descriptive, domain-appropriate labels validated through card sorting or tree testing.
- Use established generic patterns—such as a top bar, sidebar, breadcrumbs, stepper, search, and conventional action placement—only where they fit the task.
- Develop an independent visual system from your brand: typography, spacing, color, iconography, component states, and motion. Document the rationale and design provenance.
- Prototype responsive behavior explicitly for narrow, medium, and wide viewports, including navigation collapse, content priority, touch targets, keyboard use, focus order, error handling, and reduced motion.
- Test task completion and comprehension with representative users. Measure success rate, time on task, recovery from errors, and label comprehension rather than subjective similarity to the competitor.

Before design begins, provide the product’s primary users, top workflows, brand guidance, platform targets, and accessibility requirement. If competitor research is later approved, treat it as one input among several: record only functional observations, avoid copying screenshots or distinctive wording, and have legal or the designated rights owner review any questionable similarities.

The deliverable should be an original responsive workflow, component inventory, breakpoint behavior, content model, accessibility notes, and a short decision log showing that the design arose from user needs and shared conventions—not imitation.
